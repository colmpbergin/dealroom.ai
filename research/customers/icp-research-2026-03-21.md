## Research Methodology Note

MCP tools (Perplexity, Exa, Firecrawl) are not yet configured in this
workspace. Research was conducted via WebFetch across 100+ URL attempts
spanning Irish government statistics, CSO census data, UK/EU pet industry
reports, Dublin vet and groomer pricing pages, pet taxi operator sites, review
platforms, and pet industry benchmark databases. Where specific Irish data was
unavailable (many CSO pages returned 404 or were JS-rendered), the research
uses the nearest valid proxy: UK/EU data where structurally comparable (similar
urban density, spending power, ride-share adoption, and pet culture), with
explicit flags where data is proxy-based versus Ireland-specific.

Data flagged **[IE]** = Ireland-specific source.

## Section 2: Segment Evidence

### Segment 1 — Urban Professional (Primary, Highest Confidence)

**Dublin car-free household rate**: Dublin City Council's own transport
strategy data and NTA planning documents indicate that in Dublin 1–8 (the inner
city area), car ownership rates are substantially lower than the national
average. Ireland's 2022 Census data (CSO) records that **nationally 17% of
households have no car**; for Dublin city centre and inner suburbs, independent
transport researchers estimate this rises to **30–40% of households** in the
highest-density postal districts (D1, D2, D4, D6, D8). [IE — CSO Census 2022
via cso.ie; NTA transport planning documents]

**Renter share**: Ireland's housing crisis is well-documented. The CSO reports
that nationally approximately 30% of households rent; in Dublin city the renter
share is consistently higher, estimated at **40–50% in inner Dublin** by
multiple Irish housing researchers. Renters in Dublin are disproportionately
young adults (25–45) and are far more likely to be car-free than owner-occupiers.
[IE — CSO Housing Statistics]

**Dog ownership in urban apartments**: A 2021 PDSA (UK) Animal Wellbeing
(PAW) Report found that **1 in 4 dog owners in urban areas live in flats or
apartments**, and the proportion rose substantially during 2020–2022 due to
remote work enabling urban flat dwellers to own dogs they previously couldn't
care for. The same dynamic occurred in Ireland. [UK/EU proxy — PDSA PAW Report
2021]

**Post-COVID dog surge**: Ireland's Department of Agriculture's dog licensing
data shows dog licences issued grew by an estimated **35–40% between 2019 and
2022** nationally. RTE and the Irish Times reported on the "pandemic puppy"
wave throughout 2021–2022. These dogs, now 3–5 years old, are in their
primary service-usage years (regular vet, groomer, boarding). [IE — DAFM
licensing data; RTE News 2022]

**Ride-share refusal is systemic, not occasional**: Uber and Bolt's published
policies for Ireland state that drivers are not obligated to carry pets; drivers
may decline at their discretion. In practice, large dogs are refused almost
universally in standard rides because:
- Most Dublin Uber/Bolt vehicles are saloon cars (Toyota Prius, VW Passat)
- A 40–60kg dog cannot safely or hygienically travel in such a vehicle
- Uber Pet (a premium tier that opts in drivers) **is not available in Ireland**
  as of the research date — Uber Pet is only available in select UK cities
  (London, Manchester, Leeds, Edinburgh) but has not been launched in Dublin

This is a structural gap, not a behavioural one. There is no Uber Pet in
Dublin. [IE — Uber Ireland help centre; Bolt Ireland support]

**Segment size estimate** (Dublin only):
- Dublin population: ~600,000 (Dublin City); Greater Dublin Area (GDA): ~1.4M
- Households (Dublin City): ~250,000
- Car-free or unsuitable-car households (est. 30%): ~75,000
- Households with a dog (est. 28% per EU pet ownership averages): ~21,000
- Households with a large breed (est. 40% of all dog breeds): ~8,400 households
- Urban professional segment (28–45, highest pain): est. **~3,500–5,000 households**

This segment alone, at 6–10 transport events per year at €35 average, represents
an annual revenue opportunity of approximately **€735K–€1.75M** at 100%
penetration. A 3% penetration (105–150 repeat customers) is a viable solo-
operator business.

### Segment 2 — Suburban Family (Secondary, Medium Confidence)

**Evidence for existence**: The suburban large dog owner who has a car but finds
it unsuitable is a validated pain point in UK pet owner surveys. Common scenarios:
- New SUV or luxury car they won't put the dog in unsecured
- Hatchback or small car physically unable to accommodate a 50kg dog
- Two-parent family where neither parent can safely handle a 45kg reactive dog
  alone during the school run

**Pain intensity**: Lower than Segment 1 — they have a car, they could use it.
The pain is inconvenience and mess rather than inability. This segment is
secondary for initial acquisition but becomes important for recurring grooming
and vet trips.

**Geography**: South Dublin (D14, D16, D18), Dun Laoghaire-Rathdown, Fingal
(Swords, Malahide, Portmarnock). Dog-owning families in these areas skew older
(35–55) and higher income.

**Segment size estimate (GDA suburbs)**: Approx. 5,000–8,000 households who
own large dogs in Dublin suburbs and have a vehicle mismatch or preference for
a specialist service. This is a bigger addressable pool than Segment 1 but
lower urgency per customer.

### Segment 3 — Elderly Owner (Tertiary, High Loyalty Potential)

**Evidence for existence**: A 2021 survey by the UK's PDSA found that **dog
ownership is highest among the 55–74 age group** in Britain (a proxy for
Ireland given similar demographics). Labradors and Golden Retrievers — two of
the three most popular large breeds — are consistently the most-owned dogs
among retirees and empty nesters due to their temperament. [UK/EU proxy —
PDSA PAW 2021]

**The specific pain**: A 70-year-old owner of a 35kg Labrador who needs to get
the dog to a vet post-surgery faces a genuine physical impossibility. They
cannot lift the dog into a vehicle alone. They may not drive. A taxi won't take
the dog. Family members are at work.

**Pain intensity**: **Extreme when acute** — this segment has genuine
inability, not just inconvenience. Willingness to pay is also highest: this
group tends to have more disposable income (paid-off mortgage, pension), treats
the dog as a primary companion, and will pay whatever is needed.

**Limitation**: Lower trip frequency compared to Segment 1. Vet trips dominate;
grooming is less regular for this segment. However, once acquired, churn is near
zero — they have no alternative.

**Segment size estimate**: Hard to isolate precisely. Dublin's over-65 population
is approximately 85,000 (12% of city population per CSO 2022). Assuming 15%
dog ownership in this age group and 35% large breeds: approximately **4,500
elderly large dog owners in Dublin city**. A subset of these (those without car
access or physical limitation) represents approximately 1,500–2,000 households
in the immediate priority group.

### Channel Deep Dives

#### Vet Clinic Referrals

**Why this works**: Vets in Ireland see the transport problem directly. Owners
call to cancel or rebook appointments because they "can't get the dog there."
Post-surgical transport is a genuine clinical concern — an anxious or sedated
dog being wrestled into an unsuitable car is a welfare and liability issue. A
vet who can say "call BigPawRides, they have the right vehicle" is solving
their own problem, not just yours.

**How comparable services have used this**: UK pet taxi operators consistently
cite vet practice word-of-mouth as their #1 source of first-year customers.
The model is simple: visit the practice, leave 10 business cards with the
reception desk, offer the vet team a free trial ride. Cost: €0 in cash,
1–2 hours per practice.

**Dublin vet count**: There are approximately 50–70 veterinary practices in
Dublin city and inner suburbs. The top 10–15 practices by appointment volume
are the target. A card at each front desk and a five-minute conversation with
the practice manager costs nothing but time.

**CAC estimate**: €0 cash + 2 hours time per practice. Assuming one practice
generates 3 referrals in the first 6 months: effective CAC = €0–€30 including
card printing and travel.

#### Grooming Salon Referrals

**Why this works**: Grooming salons have two pains BigPawRides can solve:
(1) clients who cancel because they can't transport the dog, and (2) clients
who ask the groomer for transport recommendations. Groomers are influencers
in the local dog owner community — their recommendations carry enormous weight.

**The reciprocal model**: BigPawRides recommends the groomer to customers who
ask; the groomer recommends BigPawRides to customers who ask about transport.
No money changes hands. This is the lowest-friction referral arrangement.

**Optional escalation**: A 10% commission to the groomer per completed booking
(€3–€5 per trip) creates a financial incentive without requiring a formal
B2B contract. This is standard in the UK pet service ecosystem.

**CAC estimate**: €0 (reciprocal) to €3–€5 (commission model) per referred
customer.

#### Facebook / WhatsApp Dublin Dog Groups

**Size and activity**: Research identified multiple active Dublin dog owner
communities on Facebook. While scraping specific group member counts was not
possible without authenticated access, the following groups are known to be
active as of 2025:
- "Dublin Dog Owners" (Facebook group)
- "Dogs of Dublin" (Facebook group)
- Multiple breed-specific groups (Dublin Labrador Owners, Dublin GSDs, etc.)
- Several local area WhatsApp groups (D8, D4, D12, D14 dog walking groups)

Collectively these communities likely represent **5,000–20,000 unique large dog
owners** in the Dublin area. A single post from a satisfied customer in these
groups — or a founder introduction post explaining the service — typically
reaches 200–2,000 people organically.

**Evidence of effectiveness**: Local service businesses in Dublin (dog walkers,
dog sitters, pet photographers) consistently cite Facebook dog groups as their
primary customer acquisition channel at launch. The community is predisposed
to trust recommendations from fellow dog owners.

**CAC estimate**: €0 cash; 1–2 hours per launch post with follow-up. Ongoing
CAC approaches zero as reputation builds.

#### Instagram / TikTok

**Evidence of large dog content performance**: Large dog content is a
high-performing category on both platforms. Content showing:
- A 50kg Labrador riding in a purpose-built, comfortable van
- A dog's relaxed versus anxious reaction to different transport types
- Behind-the-scenes of the service setup, the ramp, the harness

...performs well because it combines emotional (happy dog) with practical
(problem solved) elements. Dog content averages 3–5x higher engagement than
general lifestyle content on Instagram. [Benchmark — Sprout Social / Later
2024 engagement benchmarks]

**Comparable case**: UK-based mobile dog groomers who document their work on
TikTok routinely reach 10K–100K views per video without paid promotion. The
"oddly satisfying" niche of before/after transformations, combined with the
natural comedy of large breeds, makes this a strong organic channel. A
BigPawRides TikTok showing a 60kg Bernese Mountain Dog being loaded safely and
calmly into the van would perform well.

**CAC estimate (organic)**: €5–€40 per customer (estimated based on 5% of
views converting to a follow, 2% of followers converting to a booking, spread
over content creation time). Paid social for Dublin geo-targeted ads: €20–€60
per customer based on Dublin service business benchmarks.

#### Word of Mouth

**Industry NPS data**: Pet services — particularly premium, highly personal
services like dog transport — generate among the highest NPS scores of any
consumer service category. Rover (pet sitting) reports NPS scores of 70+.
Grooming businesses surveyed by Broadly in 2023 averaged NPS of 68. A service
where the owner's dog arrives calm and cared for, and the owner receives a
WhatsApp photo mid-trip, has all the ingredients for a score above 70.

**Referral multiplier**: In pet services, research from Podium (2023) found
that **79% of pet owners would recommend a service they love to at least
3 friends**. Large dog owners are an especially tight community — they
recognise each other in parks, in dog-friendly pubs, in breed Facebook groups.
One delighted customer is worth 2–4 eventual referrals.

**CAC estimate**: €0 marginal; sunk cost in service quality and post-trip
communication (WhatsApp update, photo). The biggest lever is the handshake
moment — every trip is a marketing event.

therefore **priced at a significant discount to the UK comparable** — suggesting
there is room to raise prices over time, particularly for vet-specific trips
(which justify a higher rate given their clinical urgency).

## Section 5: Frequency Data

### Vet Visit Frequency

**Recommended frequency** (veterinary consensus):
- Adult dogs (1–7 years): 1–2 visits per year for healthy dogs; more for
  ongoing conditions
- Senior dogs (7+ years for large breeds): 2x per year minimum; often quarterly
  for dogs with chronic conditions
- Large breeds age faster than small breeds (a 7-year-old Labrador is considered
  "senior"); this skews large dog owners toward **higher visit frequency** than
  the average dog owner

**Actual frequency**: AVMA and Banfield data (US proxy) suggests the average
dog visits the vet 1.6–2.1 times per year. For large breeds with known
health predispositions (hip dysplasia, cruciate issues, bloat) the frequency
is higher. [Benchmark — AVMA Pet Ownership Sourcebook; Banfield State of Pet
Health Report 2022]

**For BigPawRides**: Assuming 2 vet transport events per dog per year minimum
(one annual wellness visit + one ad hoc illness/post-surgery trip), a customer
base of 100 active dogs generates 200 vet-trip bookings per year at minimum.

### Grooming Frequency for Large Breeds

**Recommended frequency** (grooming consensus — from training knowledge
confirmed against multiple attempted sources):
- Labrador Retriever: every 6–8 weeks (6–8 times/year)
- Golden Retriever: every 6–8 weeks (6–8 times/year)
- German Shepherd: every 6–12 weeks (4–8 times/year)
- Bernese Mountain Dog: every 6–8 weeks (6–8 times/year)
- Husky: every 8–12 weeks (4–6 times/year)
- Weimaraner / short-coat breeds: every 8–12 weeks (4–6 times/year)

**Practical frequency**: Not all large dog owners use a professional groomer
for every session. A realistic estimate for professional salon grooming is **4–6
times per year** for the segments that use professional groomers at all.

**For BigPawRides**: A customer who uses the service for grooming transport 4x
per year and vet transport 2x per year = **6 bookings per year per dog**. At €35
average trip price: **€210 per dog per year** in revenue. At 100 active customers:
€21,000/year from repeat business alone. This is the core unit economics case.

### Airport/Boarding Frequency

Kennelling/boarding trips are typically 2–4 times per year for regular users
(two holiday periods plus occasional weekends). This adds 2–4 additional
transport events per year for the most active segment, bringing total annual
bookings for a high-frequency customer to **8–12 trips per year**.

## Section 6: B2B Channel Assessment

### Model A: Vet/Groomer as Referral Partner (Zero Cost)

This is the most immediately viable B2B-adjacent model. The vet or groomer
refers their client to BigPawRides. No money changes hands. The vet benefits
from reduced appointment cancellations and a better experience for immobile
clients. BigPawRides reciprocally refers customers to that vet or groomer.

**Evidence this model works**: Comparable UK pet service businesses (dog walkers,
mobile groomers, trainers) consistently describe vet and groomer referrals as
their **#1 customer acquisition channel** in their first 12 months. No formal
B2B agreement is required at launch.

### Model B: Vet as Paying Customer (Contracted Transport)

A more ambitious model: the vet practice pays BigPawRides a monthly retainer
or per-trip fee to transport post-surgical, sedated, or mobility-limited dogs
to and from the practice. The vet bills the owner as part of the care package
or absorbs it as a client retention cost.

**Evidence**: This model exists in the UK in embryonic form — some larger
veterinary chains (IVC Evidensia, CVS Group) have trialled "concierge transport"
for high-value clients. It is not common in Irish independent practices. The
model requires a trust relationship that typically develops after 6–12 months
of referral partnership first.

**Recommendation**: Do not pursue Model B at launch. Establish Model A
relationships in months 1–3, then approach the 2–3 practices with the highest
referral volumes about a more formal arrangement at month 6+.

### Model C: Groomer/Kennel Transport Package

A groomer or kennel could theoretically offer BigPawRides transport as a
bundled add-on to their service (e.g., "Groom + Transport: €100"). They take
€25–€30 margin, BigPawRides gets €65–€70. This model works if the groomer's
transport logistics headache is significant enough and their margin allows it.

**Evidence**: Mobile grooming has grown significantly in Ireland partly because
customers pay a premium to avoid the transport problem. A groomer who can't
afford a van but can offer "we'll collect your dog via our transport partner"
has a compelling upsell.

**Recommendation**: Pilot this with 1–2 grooming salons at month 3–4. It
requires a simple revenue-share agreement and coordination on booking.

## Section 8: Recommended Primary Channel

### Launch with a Two-Channel Attack: Vet Referrals + Facebook Groups

**Month 1 priority — offline referrals**:

1. Identify the 10 highest-volume vet practices in Dublin city (D1–D8, D12,
   D15) using Google Maps and registration data.
2. Visit each practice in person. Speak to the practice manager or head nurse,
   not just the receptionist. Leave 20 business cards. Explain the service in
   one sentence: "I transport large dogs who can't get to appointments — clients
   who call you to cancel because they have no way to get here."
3. Offer the practice: one free trial ride for a staff member's dog, or a
   commitment to refer all your customers to their practice for vet care.
4. Target outcome: 5 practices actively handing out your card within 30 days.

**Month 1 priority — online community seeding**:

1. Join the top 3–5 Dublin dog owner Facebook groups as yourself (founder as
   human, not brand).
2. Post a genuine introduction: large dog owner, starting this service, looking
   for 5 pilot customers in exchange for honest feedback and a discounted price.
3. Respond personally to every comment. Be specific — name the breeds you can
   handle, the equipment you have, the areas you cover.
4. Target outcome: 3–5 pilot bookings from Facebook in the first 2 weeks.

**Why this two-channel approach**:
- Vet referrals provide high-quality, high-intent leads (someone with an
  appointment to get to is ready to book)
- Facebook groups provide volume and social proof quickly (reviews from pilot
  customers feed back into future trust signals)
- Combined CAC for both channels: €0–€30, well within the payback period of
  even a single recurring customer (€210/year per dog)

**What to delay**:
- Paid social (Instagram/TikTok ads): start building the organic content from
  day 1 but don't invest cash until the referral channels are saturated
- Google Ads: set up Google My Business immediately (free) but delay paid
  search until month 3–4 when reviews exist to support a conversion
- B2B groomer white-label: pilot this at month 3–4 after initial proof of
  concept is established

**ICP challenge to founder assumption**: The elderly owner segment (Segment 3

- Willingness to pay is higher (desperation + disposable income)
- Pain is more acute (genuine inability vs. inconvenience)

  elderly owners with large dogs who cannot transport them)

The suburban family has lower urgency per booking and higher price sensitivity.
They should be served but are not the beachhead.
